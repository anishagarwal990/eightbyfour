## Why a Structured BOQ Matters

A bill of quantities is only useful if it's specific enough to price and stock-check without a round of clarifying questions. A list that says "plywood, laminate, some hardware" tells a supplier almost nothing — grade, thickness and finish are what actually determine price and availability, and without them every line item turns into a back-and-forth conversation before a real quote can even start. A well-structured BOQ, by contrast, gets priced and stock-checked in one pass.

## What to Include Per Category

**Plywood and boards (MDF/HDHMR):** category (plywood, MDF, HDHMR), grade (MR, BWP, BWR, HDHMR), thickness, and quantity in sheets. If the application matters for grade selection (kitchen carcass vs. dry wardrobe interior), note it — it helps catch a spec mismatch before ordering, not after.

**Laminates and veneers:** brand or finish family if you already have a preference, thickness, and sheet count. If you're still deciding on finish, note that explicitly rather than leaving the line blank — it tells the supplier to treat it as an open item, not a missed one.

**Hardware (hinges, channels, handles):** type and quantity per unit (e.g., "soft-close hinges — 24 pairs" for a specific wardrobe), not just a lump "hardware" line. Hardware is the category most often under-specified, since it's easy to defer until the carcass work is decided — but ordering it late is also the most common cause of a stalled final-fit stage.

**Adhesives:** type (e.g., synthetic resin, PU) and pack size. This is the line most BOQs skip entirely, since it's a small-ticket item next to sheet materials — but a project genuinely cannot proceed without it, and a missed adhesive line means a delivery has to be re-scheduled just for it.

## Structuring the Document

The clearest BOQs group by room or unit, then by material within that group — a wardrobe's line items together (carcass board, shutter finish, hardware, adhesive), rather than one long undifferentiated materials list. This structure does two things: it makes it easy to spot a missing item (a wardrobe listed with a carcass board but no hardware line is an obvious gap once grouped this way), and it makes partial delivery and phased ordering far easier to track against the original document.

## Common Mistakes That Cause Delay

- **Materials without specs.** "Plywood" alone can't be priced — grade and thickness are required, not optional detail.
- **Missing adhesive and hardware lines.** Both are easy to forget since they're not the visible finish material, but a project stalls without them.
- **No quantity buffer for cutting waste.** Sheet materials aren't used at 100% efficiency — a typical buffer is worth building in rather than ordering the exact calculated minimum and running short mid-installation.
- **Finalising a spec before confirming current stock.** A shade or finish can move in and out of stock — confirm availability before it's locked into architectural drawings, not after.
- **One combined list for a multi-phase project.** If a project delivers in phases (carcass work, then finishing, then fixtures), a single flat BOQ makes it hard to track what's been ordered against what's still pending — phase-tagging each line item avoids that confusion later.
